What does a labor-market recovery mean?

Overall labor-force participation in 2024 was 62.9%, 0.5 percentage points lower than its 2019 rate of 63.4%. Yet participation among adults aged 25 to 54 was 1.0 percentage points above its 2019 level. Did participation fall within age groups, or did the population’s age mix change? The answer changes how we interpret the apparent shortfall in the labor-market recovery.

Unlike unemployment, which counts jobseekers as a share of the labor force, participation counts workers and jobseekers as a share of the population. I use monthly IPUMS CPS microdata for civilians aged 16 and older from 2019 through 2024, apply WTFINL sampling weights to represent the population, and average the twelve monthly rates for each annual estimate.

First, compare recovery paths

Figure 1 shows changes from 2019 across four stages of working life. By 2024, participation among adults aged 25 to 54 was 1.0 percentage points above its baseline. Adults aged 55 to 64 gained 0.7 points, while those aged 16 to 24 were approximately unchanged, gaining 0.1 points. Adults aged 65 and older were the only group still below baseline, down 0.8 points.

All four groups declined between 2019 and 2020. Adults aged 16 to 24 had the deepest drop, 2.4 percentage points. Their participation rebounded above baseline in 2023 before falling back toward it in 2024. The contrasting outcomes for adults aged 55 to 64 and those aged 65 and older show why treating all older adults as one group would hide part of the recovery.

Next, look at who makes up the population

Figure 2 introduces the other part of the story. Adults aged 65 and older grew from 20.4% to 22.2% of the population in this analysis. The shares of the three younger groups fell. This matters because participation in 2024 was 19.9% among adults aged 65 and older, compared with 83.7% among those aged 25 to 54. A growing share of people in a group with lower participation can pull down the overall rate even when participation improves within most groups.

Finally, hold the age mix fixed

To separate these two influences, I calculate each month’s overall rate twice. Let \(s_{g,y,m}\) be age group \(g\)’s population share and \(p_{g,y,m}\) its participation rate in year \(y\), month \(m\).

\[ P_{y,m}=\sum_g s_{g,y,m}p_{g,y,m} \qquad\text{(observed)} \]

\[ P^{\mathrm{fixed}}_{y,m}=\sum_g s_{g,2019,m}p_{g,y,m} \qquad\text{(2019 age mix)} \]

The second calculation keeps the same participation rates but substitutes the corresponding month’s 2019 population shares. For example, January 2024 uses January 2019 shares. I then average each set of twelve monthly rates. Only the shares differ, so the gap isolates the composition effect under this calculation.

Figure 3 expresses both series as changes from their common 2019 baseline. With the age mix held fixed, participation in 2024 was 0.4 percentage points higher than in 2019. The observed shortfall becomes a modest increase.

In levels, observed participation in 2024 was 62.86%, compared with 63.85% using the 2019 age mix. The gap was 0.99 percentage points. That difference describes the contribution of changing age shares under this standardization.

Takeaway and limits

The CPS has a complex rotating design, and pandemic-era collection disruptions complicate interpretation. These comparisons do not establish causes or statistical significance. Annual averages hide monthly changes, and broad age groups hide differences within them. This analysis does not follow a fixed cohort.

  • Participation recovered differently across age groups, with adults aged 65 and older still below their 2019 rate.
  • The population also shifted toward that older group, which has much lower participation.
  • With the 2019 age mix held fixed, 2024 participation was 0.4 percentage points higher than in 2019, instead of 0.5 percentage points lower. Under this standardization, the shift toward older age groups more than accounts for the overall shortfall.

Data: Flood et al. (2025), IPUMS CPS: Version 13.0, University of Minnesota. Basic Monthly samples, January 2019 through December 2024; extract created September 27, 2026. The analysis uses 6,146,546 eligible person-month observations, not unique individuals.
