How much did the labor market weaken?

U.S. inflation fell sharply after its 2022 peak. How much did unemployment rise during that decline, and when? This question relates to the Phillips curve, which connects inflation and unemployment but does not establish a fixed policy tradeoff. For workers, an endpoint comparison can conceal whether a weakening labor market accompanied the entire adjustment or only its later part.

I examine 2015 through 2024, using the earlier years as context for the adjustment after 2022. The charts suggest an early period of substantial inflation decline with little unemployment movement, followed by a later rise in unemployment. The size of that rise depends on the starting date.

Data and calculations

I downloaded BLS series CPIAUCSL and UNRATE programmatically with R’s download.file() and FRED’s public CSV URLs. The repository includes acquisition and analysis code, saved data, and replication instructions. Its --refresh option downloads updated observations; the default reproduces the saved snapshot.

Both series are seasonally adjusted. Unemployment is the percentage of the civilian labor force unemployed. Inflation is 100 × (CPI this month / CPI twelve months earlier − 1). An extra year supplies the initial CPI comparison, leaving 120 matched months. Falling positive inflation means prices increase more slowly, not that they fall. Using seasonally adjusted CPI can produce small differences from annual headline rates calculated with unadjusted CPI.

When did the two rates turn?

Figure 1 focuses on 2022 through 2024. Its aligned panels show whether inflation’s decline began at the same time as unemployment’s rise.

Inflation peaked at 9.0% in June 2022, when unemployment was 3.6%. By December 2024, the rates were 2.9% and 4.1%. That is a 6.1 percentage point inflation decline alongside a 0.5 point unemployment rise.

However, unemployment first fell to 3.4% in April 2023. Measured from that trough, its increase was 0.7 points. Neither baseline is inherently wrong. The first describes movement since peak inflation; the second measures labor market weakening from the subsequent unemployment low.

Did similar unemployment mean similar inflation?

Those turning points invite a broader comparison. Figure 2 pairs the rates in each month, with colors and shapes separating 2015 through 2019 (before COVID), 2020 and 2021 (disruption), and 2022 through 2024 (inflation and cooling).

Unemployment was 3.6% in December 2019 and 3.6% in June 2022, but inflation was 2.3% and 9.0%, respectively. Similar unemployment did not imply similar inflation. This cautions against interpreting all the points as one stable relationship. The scatterplot, however, hides time ordering within each group, which the final chart restores.

How did the adjustment unfold?

Figure 3 connects consecutive quarters. Averaging three monthly rates makes the path readable but hides monthly fluctuations. Downward movement means falling inflation; rightward movement means rising unemployment. The horizontal axis is narrower than Figure 2’s, so visual distances are not directly comparable.

Choosing an unemployment trough would partly build a subsequent rise into the comparison. Instead, I select the first quarter when inflation completed at least half its decline from the quarterly peak to 2024 Q4. This uses inflation alone. The threshold is 5.7%, first crossed in 2023 Q2. It remains a retrospective rule dependent on the endpoint, not an estimated structural break.

From 2022 Q2 to 2023 Q2, inflation fell from 8.6% to 4.1%, while unemployment moved from 3.6% to 3.5%. By 2024 Q4, unemployment was 4.1% and inflation was 2.7%, a further inflation decline of 1.3 points. Differences use unrounded values, so subtraction of displayed rates can round differently.

Using 40% or 60% of the inflation decline instead gives comparison quarters from 2023 Q1 to 2023 Q2. The sensitivity table reports the results. All three thresholds show little early unemployment movement and a larger later increase.

Unemployment estimates have sampling uncertainty. A change of a tenth of a point should not be treated as a precise population movement. I have not calculated standard errors for these quarterly comparisons and make no claim of statistical significance.

Takeaway and limits

Much of the inflation decline preceded the clearer rise in unemployment. In Phillips curve terms, the observed path does not resemble a fixed exchange of one percentage point of unemployment for each point of lower inflation. This does not reject the broader Phillips curve framework. The unemployment increase is also larger when measured from its trough rather than from peak inflation.

These charts cannot identify a policy’s causal effect. Supply conditions, demand, expectations, and policy can all affect the relationship. National averages also conceal differences across workers and households. The finding describes this episode, not a universal rule about the cost of reducing inflation.
