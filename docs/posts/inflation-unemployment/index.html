A trade-off that changes with time

Does bringing inflation down require a large increase in unemployment? The question matters because price stability and access to jobs both affect living standards. A simple inflation–unemployment trade-off suggests that a weaker labor market should accompany slower price growth. But a chart showing two variables moving together cannot establish how much unemployment a policy must create to reduce inflation.

I examine U.S. monthly data from 2015 through 2024, comparing the years before COVID, the disruption in 2020–2021, and inflation’s rise and retreat in 2022–2024. This fixed window provides a pre-pandemic benchmark and keeps the comparison separate from later developments. The central finding is that the relationship changed substantially across these periods.

Measuring the two rates

The data come from the Bureau of Labor Statistics through FRED: CPIAUCSL, the all-items consumer price index, and UNRATE, the unemployment rate. Both series are seasonally adjusted. Unemployment measures unemployed people as a share of the civilian labor force, not the entire population.

I calculate inflation as 100 × (CPI this month / CPI twelve months earlier − 1). An extra year of CPI data supplies the initial comparison, leaving 120 matched monthly observations. This is a growth rate, rather than the CPI’s index level: falling positive inflation means prices are increasing more slowly, not necessarily falling. Because the calculation uses seasonally adjusted CPI, it can differ slightly from the commonly reported year-over-year rate based on unadjusted CPI.

First, establish the sequence

knitr::include_graphics(file.path(POST_DIR, "results/figures/timeline.png"))

Figure 1 shows unemployment reaching 14.8% in April 2020, when inflation was only 0.3%. Inflation instead peaked at 9.0% in June 2022, with unemployment at 3.6%. These peaks describe different stages of the episode, rather than one simultaneous shock.

By December 2024, inflation was 2.9% and unemployment was 4.1%. Relative to the inflation peak, inflation declined 6.1 percentage points while unemployment rose 0.5 points. The endpoint comparison is striking, but the intervening path matters too.

Next, compare relationships across periods

knitr::include_graphics(file.path(POST_DIR, "results/figures/scatter.png"))

Figure 2 shows why one fitted line would hide important differences. Before COVID, relatively low unemployment coexisted with modest inflation. During 2020–2021, high-unemployment months had low inflation, while inflation rose as unemployment receded. In 2022–2024, inflation varied widely within a much narrower unemployment range.

For a concrete comparison, unemployment was 3.6% in December 2019 and 3.6% in June 2022, but inflation was 2.3% and 9.0%, respectively. Unemployment alone does not summarize the inflation environment.

Finally, follow the disinflation path

knitr::include_graphics(file.path(POST_DIR, "results/figures/path.png"))

Figure 3 restores the time ordering lost in the scatterplot. Between 2022 Q2 and 2023 Q2, average inflation fell from 8.6% to 4.1%, while unemployment moved from 3.6% to 3.5%. Much of that initial disinflation occurred without rising unemployment. By 2024 Q4, unemployment had increased to 4.1%, with inflation at 2.7%. The later labor-market cooling should not be erased by focusing only on the earlier improvement.

Takeaway and limits

These charts do not reveal a stable, mechanical trade-off. Inflation fell substantially after its peak without unemployment returning anywhere near its pandemic high. However, that does not show disinflation is always costless or that a particular policy caused the outcome. Supply conditions, demand, expectations, and policy may all affect the relationship; these two series cannot separate their contributions. Nor does this analysis track individual workers or household-specific prices.

The takeaway is to examine timing and economic context before treating a correlation as a policy menu. Lower inflation and a relatively strong labor market coexisted during much of this recovery, even as unemployment edged upward later.
